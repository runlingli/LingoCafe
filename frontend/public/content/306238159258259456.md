French spelling and French pronunciation are two entirely different systems. A word like *beaucoup* has six letters but only three sounds. Understanding which letters are silent — and when they become audible through liaison — is one of the most important early steps in sounding natural.

## Silent Final Consonants

Most final consonants in French are **not pronounced**. The letters -s, -t, -d, -x, -z at the end of a word are silent in isolation:

| Word | Spelling | Pronunciation |
|------|----------|---------------|
| *vous* | v-o-u-s | /vu/ |
| *grand* | g-r-a-n-d | /ɡʁɑ̃/ |
| *beaux* | b-e-a-u-x | /bo/ |
| *nez* | n-e-z | /ne/ |

The major exceptions: **C, R, F, L** are usually pronounced at the end of a word — remember the English word **CaReFuL**:

- *parc* → /paʁk/
- *mer* → /mɛʁ/
- *bref* → /bʁɛf/
- *journal* → /ʒuʁnal/

## Liaison

Liaison occurs when a normally silent final consonant is **pronounced** because the next word begins with a vowel or a silent *h*. The consonant "links" into the following syllable.

| Phrase | Without liaison | With liaison |
|--------|----------------|-------------|
| *vous avez* | voo / avay | voo-**z**-avay |
| *ils ont* | eel / on | eel-**z**-on |
| *est ici* | eh / ee-see | eh-**t**-ee-see |
| *les hommes* | lay / om | lay-**z**-om |

Note that **-s and -x** become a /z/ sound in liaison. **-d** becomes /t/. **-n** is fully nasalised in liaison.

### Obligatory liaison (always do it)
- Article + noun: *les enfants*
- Pronoun + verb: *nous allons*, *ils ont*
- After common adverbs: *très agréable*, *bien ici*

### Forbidden liaison (never do it)
- After *et*: ~~*et il*~~ → always silent
- Before *h aspiré*: *les haricots* (no liaison — the h is "aspirate")
- After a singular noun: *un enfant adorable* (no liaison between *enfant* and *adorable*)

## The Silent E

The letter *e* at the end of a word is generally silent: *table*, *mange*, *forte*. In rapid speech, unstressed *e* inside a word also tends to disappear: *samedi* → /sam-di/, *acheter* → /ash-tay/.

Practise by listening to native speakers at normal speed — radio, podcasts, films — and you will begin to hear the patterns intuitively rather than needing to apply rules consciously.
