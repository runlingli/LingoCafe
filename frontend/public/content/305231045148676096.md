# French Verb Conjugation: Mastering Être and Avoir

Every French sentence ultimately depends on one of two verbs: **être** (to be) and **avoir** (to have). They are irregular, so their conjugations must be memorised rather than derived from a pattern — but that effort pays dividends immediately, because these two verbs appear in virtually every tense and construction you will encounter.

## Present Tense

| Pronom | être | avoir |
|--------|------|-------|
| je | suis | ai |
| tu | es | as |
| il / elle | est | a |
| nous | sommes | avons |
| vous | êtes | avez |
| ils / elles | sont | ont |

Note that **avoir** drops the h- sound in speech: *j'ai* is said as a single syllable, and the liaison in *nous avons* is obligatory.

## As Auxiliary Verbs

Both verbs are essential as **auxiliary verbs** in compound tenses.

**Avoir** is used with most verbs to form the *passé composé*:

- *j'ai mangé* — I ate
- *tu as vu* — you saw
- *ils ont compris* — they understood

**Être** is required for:

1. **Verbs of motion and change of state**: aller, venir, partir, arriver, naître, mourir, retourner, entrer, sortir, monter, descendre, tomber, rester, passer (intransitive)
2. **All reflexive verbs**: *je me suis levé*, *elle s'est habillée*

When **être** is the auxiliary, the past participle **agrees in gender and number** with the subject:

- *elle est partie* (feminine singular)
- *ils sont partis* (masculine plural)
- *elles sont parties* (feminine plural)

## Common Mistakes

- Using *avoir* instead of *être* for reflexive verbs: ~~*j'ai levé*~~ → *je me suis levé*
- Forgetting agreement: ~~*elle est parti*~~ → *elle est partie*
- Confusing *être* and *avoir* in idiomatic expressions: *avoir faim* (to be hungry), *avoir peur* (to be afraid), *avoir raison* (to be right)

## Study Strategy

Memorise the full present-tense tables for both verbs before anything else. Return to them every day for two weeks and the patterns will become automatic — then compound tenses will feel far less daunting.
